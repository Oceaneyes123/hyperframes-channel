2. Private addressesEST 11.7s

Inside your home, each device gets a private address from the router, like one ninety two dot one sixty eight dot one dot ten. Every home network reuses these ranges.

Addresses highlight in sequence · both highlight together as a reused range.

3. Not uniqueEST 9.0s

Those addresses are not unique on the Internet. Millions of homes use the same ones, so no Internet router will deliver to them.

Packet leaves the phone with its private port · reaches the router · stops at the amber boundary.

4. Public addressEST 10.2s

On the outside, the router has one public address: two oh three dot zero dot one thirteen dot seven. That is the address the Internet sees.

Boundary clears · the public chip locks under the router and stays for the rest of the video.

5. Out it goesEST 7.8s

Your phone opens a connection to a website. Its packet leaves with a private source address and a source port.

6. Rewrite the sourceEST 9.0s

The router replaces that private source with its public address and a port of its own, and writes the pair into its table.

Packet holds at the router · chip changes 51000 → 40001 and turns cyan · row 1 is written into the table.

7. The site seesEST 8.6s

Outside, the packet carries the public address. The website sees one connection from your router, and replies to that address and port.

Packet continues through the globe · crosses the outside hop · arrives at the website while row 1's outside value stays lit.

8. A second deviceEST 7.0s

Now your laptop opens its own connection. It has a different private address and a different source port.

The laptop's label gains :52000 · its packet travels the same trunk · the table still holds only row 1.

9. One addressEST 8.2s

The router gives it the same public address, but its own outside port, so it can tell the two connections apart.

Second packet translates · row 2 fills · 40001 then 40002 highlight under the shared public value · both packets sit outside.

10. The replyEST 6.2s

A reply comes back from the website, addressed to the public address and the first port.

Reply appears at the website · crosses back through the globe · stops at the router carrying 40001.

11. Match the tableEST 8.6s

The router looks that port up in the table. It matches one entry, and the destination is rewritten back to the phone.

Row 1 highlights · a check confirms the match · the chip changes to the phone's private value and turns blue.

12. Back to your phoneEST 8.2s

The reply travels to your phone. The laptop's reply uses the other entry, so each device gets only its own answers.

Reply climbs the trunk · row 2 highlights for the laptop's path · the reply lands on the phone with a green receipt.

13. No matchEST 8.6s

What if someone on the Internet connects in first? Nothing in the table matches that port, so the router drops the packet.

Inbound packet with an unknown port reaches the router · neither row matches · a red-orange x replaces the packet.

14. Not a firewallEST 9.4s

That is not the same as a firewall. NAT drops what it cannot match in its table; a firewall decides by rules someone set.

Shield glyph appears · the table side is labelled by matching · the shield side is labelled by rules · both hold.

15. One public IPEST 6.6s

Many private devices, one public address. NAT keeps the table, so every reply finds the right device.

Addresses highlight as a set · NAT labelled on the router · both rows stay lit · the public value is highlighted last.
